The whole session in four sentences

  1. We take a model that already reads English (Qwen2.5-0.5B).
  2. We bolt on a brand-new "pick one of six" layer that knows nothing, and score it: 17%, pure guessing.
  3. We show it 6,000 complaints with the right answers and let it adjust a tiny slice of itself after every batch.
  4. We score it again on the same 3,000 complaints it never trained on: 73%. That jump is the lesson.

Everything else in the notebook is plumbing so those four things can happen on a GPU.

Meet the data

!{PY} {REPO}/scripts/peek.py --shared {SHARED} --n 3
SAY AFTER IT RUNS

"Real complaints. The XXXX is personal info the government blacked out. Which two categories do you think the model will mix up?"

(The answer comes in Step 4: credit reporting and debt collection.)

About the dataset (if anyone asks, or say it before running)

  • What it is: real complaints people filed with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), the federal agency that handles complaints about banks, lenders, credit bureaus, and payment apps. The full database has about 10 million complaints.
  • Who can use it: it's public. Anyone can download it.
  • What's in each one: the complaint written in the person's own words, plus which product it was about. Names, account numbers, and dates are blacked out as XXXX.
  • Where ours came from: the CFPB stopped including the written text in its bulk download, so we used an earlier public copy on Hugging Face (about 1.7 million complaints with text, 2015 to 2024).
  • What we kept: an even sample. 20,000 per category for training (120,000 total) and a separate 500 per category for testing (3,000 total). Equal counts mean the model can't look smart by always guessing the biggest category.
CategoryWhat kind of complaint
Credit reportingErrors on a credit report, credit repair companies
Debt collectionCollectors calling, debts you don't owe or already paid
MortgageHome loans: payments, escrow, refinancing, foreclosure
Credit cardCharges, fees, disputes, card account problems
Bank accountChecking and savings accounts
Money transfer & paymentsPayment apps, wire transfers, prepaid cards, crypto

peek.py opens train.parquet (a compressed table file, like a fast CSV), counts complaints per category, and prints 3 random ones (--n 3). Run it again, get 3 different ones.

Expected: 120,000 complaints, exactly 20,000 per category. Why equal? If one category were 50% of the data, a model could score 50% by always saying that category and look smart while learning nothing. Equal counts make 17% the honest "knows nothing" line.

XXXX and {$2900.00} are CFPB's redactions and formatting, not our bug.

What the code does: Opens the dataset, counts complaints per category, and shows a few real ones.
Why it matters for their projects: Always look at your data before you build anything. Read 20 rows. Check whether one category swamps the others. Most project problems are data problems you could have spotted here.

Score the model before training

!{PY} {REPO}/scripts/train.py --shared {SHARED} --out {RUNS}/before --epochs 0
SAY AFTER IT RUNS

"17%. With six choices, that's pure guessing. The red MISSING warning is normal: it's the brand-new answer layer that hasn't learned anything yet."

Underneath

Same script as training, but --epochs 0 means "zero passes: skip training, just take the test." Results go to $SCRATCH/inspire/before/results.json.

What train.py does here, in order:

  1. Loads Qwen2.5-0.5B from the shared folder (no internet needed).
  2. Adds a new final layer called score with six outputs, one per category. It starts as random numbers.
  3. Wraps it in LoRA (more on this in Step 3).
  4. Takes the test: 3,000 complaints, 500 per category, that are never used for training.

The red score.weight | MISSING warning is that new layer. The library is saying "this part didn't come with the download, so I made it up randomly." True, and expected. That's the whole point of the "before" score.

Expected: accuracy on 3,000 complaints it never saw: 17.0%

Why exactly 17%, and why was debt collection 96%? A random layer usually leans toward one answer. This one said "debt collection" for almost everything. So it got 96% of the 500 debt collection complaints right and almost nothing else: about 480 + 10 + 15 + 5 right out of 3,000 ≈ 510 ≈ 17%.

It's like a student who answers "C" on every multiple-choice question: perfect on the C questions, 1 in 6 overall.

What the code does: Gives the model the test with zero training, to get a starting score.
Why it matters for their projects: Measure the "before." Without a starting number, you can't prove anything you build actually helped. That's true for an AI model, a tutoring program, or a website change.
3

Fine-tune it

TRAIN_EXAMPLES = 6000     # how many labeled complaints to learn from

!{PY} {REPO}/scripts/train.py --shared {SHARED} --out {RUNS}/run1 --limit {TRAIN_EXAMPLES} --save-adapter
SAY AFTER IT RUNS

"It's reading 32 complaints at a time, checking the answers, and adjusting. Watch the loss: that's how wrong it is, and it should go down. We're using LoRA: we freeze the model and only train a small add-on, less than half a percent of it. Like sticky notes in a textbook instead of rewriting the book."

Underneath

FlagMeaning
--out {RUNS}/run1Save this run's results in a folder named run1
--limit 6000Use the first 6,000 training complaints (of 120,000)
--save-adapterAlso save the learned LoRA weights, so Step 5 can use the trained model
(not passed)Defaults: 1 epoch, batch 32, 256 tokens, learning rate 0.0002

What happens on every step (the loop in train.py):

  1. Grab 32 complaints and their correct categories.
  2. Tokenize: chop each into word pieces, cut at 256, pad short ones so all 32 are the same length (GPUs need even rectangles).
  3. Forward: the model guesses a category for each.
  4. Loss: compare guesses to the right answers; one number for "how wrong."
  5. Backward: work out which direction to nudge each trainable weight to be less wrong.
  6. Optimizer step (AdamW): nudge them a little.
  7. Repeat.

6,000 ÷ 32 = 187.5, so 187 steps (the leftover half batch is dropped).

LoRA, the one-minute version. The model has 496 million weights. Updating all of them needs a lot of memory and time. LoRA freezes all of them and adds small "correction" matrices to the attention layers (where the model decides which words matter), plus the new score layer. Only those learn: "trainable weights: 2,168,064 of 496,206,208 (0.44%)".

Analogy that works with students: you don't rewrite a textbook to teach one class; you add sticky notes. LoRA is the sticky notes. --save-adapter saves just the sticky notes (a few MB), not a whole new textbook.

Reading the loss. From the dry run:

step    25/187   loss 2.883
step    50/187   loss 1.521
step   100/187   loss 0.778
step   175/187   loss 0.315
step   187/187   loss 0.902
  • It starts above 1.8 because the random layer is confidently wrong. Plain guessing among six would be about 1.8; 2.9 means worse than guessing, confidently.
  • It drops fast: that's learning.
  • It bounces (0.315 then 0.902). Each printed loss is one batch of 32, and some batches are just harder. Watch the trend, not each number. If a student panics about the last line, this is why.

Expected: training took 27.3s (219 complaints per second) and 72.9%.

What the code does: Shows the model 6,000 complaints with the right answers and lets it adjust a small add-on after every batch of 32.
Why it matters for their projects: You don't have to build AI from scratch. Take a model that already reads, add your own labeled examples, and teach it one job. Any sorting problem works the same way: emails, survey answers, support tickets, research abstracts. If you have text and labels, you have a project.

RUN_NAME       = "exp1"

!{PY} {REPO}/scripts/train.py --shared {SHARED} --out {RUNS}/{RUN_NAME} --limit {TRAIN_EXAMPLES} --max-len {MAX_LEN} --epochs {EPOCHS}
SAY AFTER IT RUNS

"Change one number, give it a new RUN_NAME, run it. More data, shorter reading, or more passes. One change at a time so you know what caused the difference."

Underneath

Same training script, but now all four knobs are visible. Change one, keep the others, new RUN_NAME each time. Reusing a name overwrites that run's results.

KnobWhat it controlsWhat to expect
TRAIN_EXAMPLESHow many complaints to learn fromDry run: 6,000 → 73% / 27s; 24,000 → 82% / 110s; 60,000 → 83% / 264s
MAX_LENHow many tokens of each complaint it reads64 = only the opening; 512 = most of it, slower. Not tested in the dry run
EPOCHSHow many times it sees each complaint2 = twice as long. Not tested in the dry run

The pattern to land: time grows in a straight line with data (4x data ≈ 4x time), but accuracy gains shrink (+9 points, then +1). More data helps, and each extra point costs more. If more data is better but slow, the next move is more GPUs.

Why one change at a time: if they change data and epochs and it improves, they can't say which one did it. That's the "controlled experiment" bullet at the end of the notebook.

Note: this cell has no --save-adapter, so Step 5 still uses run1. To test a bigger model on their own complaints, add --save-adapter here and change run1 to their RUN_NAME in Step 5.

What the code does: Runs the same training again with one setting changed, so you can see what that setting does.
Why it matters for their projects: Change one thing at a time, or you won't know what caused the difference. More data helped, but each extra point cost more time. Deciding how much accuracy is worth how much time is a real decision in every project.

How the notebook is wired

Read this once and the syntax stops being scary. The notebook itself does almost no machine learning. It calls Python scripts in the repo's scripts/ folder and shows you what they print. That is the one idea that makes every code cell readable.

Two pieces of Jupyter syntax do all of it:

  • ! at the start of a line means "run this in the terminal, not in Python." So !nvidia-smi is the same as typing nvidia-smi at a shell prompt.
  • {SOMETHING} inside a ! line means "paste in the Python variable named SOMETHING." So !{PY} {REPO}/scripts/peek.py becomes something like /work/.../venv/bin/python /home/.../hpc-finetune-workshop/scripts/peek.py.

The --word value parts after a script name are flags: settings handed to the script, the same way sbatch takes --nodes 4. Each script reads its flags at the top (ap.add_argument(...) in the code), and any flag you don't pass uses its default.

Questions students actually ask

QuestionShort answer
Is this ChatGPT?Same family of technology, much smaller (0.5 billion weights vs. hundreds of billions), and we turned it into a sorter, not a chatbot.
Did it memorize the answers?No. The 3,000 test complaints are never in training. That's why the score means something.
Why does it need a GPU?Each step is millions of multiplications. A GPU does thousands at once.
Why didn't we train all the weights?Slower, more memory, and for one narrow job, LoRA gets most of the benefit at 0.44% of the cost.
Why is my number different from my neighbor's?Training shuffles the data randomly, so runs land close but not identical.
Why did loss go up at the end?Each loss is one batch; some batches are harder. Look at the trend.
Can it say "none of these"?No. It was only ever given six choices.
Could it get to 100%?Unlikely. Some complaints genuinely fit two categories; people would disagree on them too.
